## The short version

You asked whether m ≥ 21 holds up. Working blind — the statement and nothing
else — I independently got to **m ≥ 19**. Two rungs short.

That is not a failure and it is not a confirmation. It is the useful outcome: the
entire risk in the claim now sits on exactly two numbers, m = 19 and m = 20, and
everything below them has been re-derived by an argument that shares no machinery
with the chain you asked me to check.

## What "m ≥ 19" means here

A counterexample to Ryser at r=6 would be a 6-partite intersecting hypergraph
needing 6 vertices to stab every edge. I proved no such thing exists with 18 edges
or fewer. The proof is a certificate: `python3 verify.py`, 96 seconds, 22 checks,
no solver, no installs, nothing to trust but arithmetic you can re-run.

Two versions, and the difference is deliberate:

- **m ≥ 18 citing nothing at all.** Every input proved inside the certificate.
- **m ≥ 19** if you grant one published constant, f(6) = 13 (Aharoni–Barát–Wanless).
  The certificate prints that dependency and says what the floor drops to without
  it, so you can always see how much rests on literature.

## How it works, briefly

Two facts, both easy once stated. First: if you delete any k vertices from a
counterexample, what survives still needs a lot of covering — so no small set of
vertices can be too popular. That caps how lopsided the degrees can get. Second:
every pair of edges has to meet somewhere, and popular vertices are the only way
to make many pairs meet at once. Put the cap against the demand and small
hypergraphs simply cannot supply enough meetings.

Run that upward from the trivial case and it climbs: 1, 3, 5, 8, ≥12, ≥18. The
low rungs are computed exhaustively — each proved twice, once by exhibiting an
object and once by exhausting everything smaller.

## The near-miss you should know about

Before computing the low rungs I guessed two of them, from a valid but loose
argument. Both guesses were too high. Had I used them, the ladder would have
returned **20** — one short of 21, and I would have reported it as near-total
independent confirmation of the very claim I was checking.

The wrong answer pointed straight at the expected answer. That is the failure mode
this whole sealed setup exists to catch, and it caught it on the founding day —
against my own work, before any of it was written down as a result. It is now a
standing rule in the repo: ladder inputs get computed, never inferred from the
shape of the answer you are hoping for.
