# Turn 8 — the floor is 22, and the risk table we published was wrong

*For JD. Plain language. Ends with the one command.*

## The headline

**A Ryser r=6 counterexample now needs at least 22 edges, still citing nothing.**
Certificate 0008, green under a bare `/usr/bin/python3` (3.9.6) in 123 seconds
and under `python3 -O` in 124.

The constraint that did it is the degree-two cap: in a counterexample, **every
edge contains at most one vertex of degree 2**, so twice the number of degree-2
vertices is at most the number of edges. That kills m = 21, which certificate
0007 had left standing.

## Three things I want you to read before the good news

**1. The lemma is not ours.** It is clause (iii) of a 2017 lemma of Francetić,
Herke, McKay and Wanless. We re-derived it here in full so the floor cites
nothing — and the re-derivation is genuinely stronger than the published clause,
because it never uses linearity and a counterexample must be non-linear. But
re-deriving a published lemma is a different act from finding one. The same is
already true of our lemmas (A) and (B), which are that paper's 2.1(ii) and (i).
**What is new here is the floor, not the mathematics under it.**

**2. It buys one rung and stops.** m = 22 does not die — 56,592 configurations
survive. Reporting only the m = 21 kill would be exactly the rounding-up the
No-Noise Law forbids, so: this lever is spent.

**3. It nearly went in on the wrong ladder.** The notebook's original table was
computed using the literature constant f(6) = 13. Had I built the floor on those
numbers, it would have silently re-imported the citation and undone certificate
0007's entire point. The citation-free run is a different, ~7× larger problem —
316,591 configurations at m = 21 rather than 43,875. It gives the same answer,
but it had to be run.

## The second job: we were wrong about what holds the result up

You asked me to attack (L7) and the excess budget X — the two steps our own risk
decomposition called the joint load-bearer and our own attack list never
mentioned. That turned up a defect in the risk decomposition itself.

**PLAN.md and D-019 said: remove (L7) and 100% of configurations survive.** That
is false. Measured with our own code, one line changed: 3 of 105 survive at
m = 20, 1,616 of 7,159 on the weak ladder, 1 of 33 at m = 19. Not 100% — 2.9%.
Five independent implementations agree.

**And the step actually doing the work has no name anywhere in the repo.** It is
the convexity bound on B_min. It is in no table, no ledger line, no certificate
label. We spent two turns ranking a step that costs at most one rung above a step
we had never written down.

Two smaller corrections from the same pass: a failure of N(4) = 9 drops the floor
to **19**, not 20 as published; and **g(4) = 8** carries a margin of exactly one
while appearing on no attack list.

**What survived.** Most of the chain, and convincingly: the identities and bounds
inside (L8) were brute-forced over 1.86 million audits with zero failures, and
the property that actually matters — that no real object's true point falls
outside any of the 16 guards — was checked directly on 1,924 instances built from
real objects. (L7)'s own derivation is sound; I verified it by hand and found a
cleaner one-line argument than the one in our notes.

## A lever closed, and a bug fixed

The tightening I flagged as the next thing to try — replacing g(4)=8 with N(4)=9
inside (L7) — is **dead**, and dead for an interesting reason: at the only
residual size where the extra unit would matter, our own corrected AKP Lemma 2.8
proves the residual cannot have the property the tightening needs. It would not
have been enough anyway.

Separately, `lib/ryser.py` was emitting hypergraphs with **repeated edges** —
`generate(3,3,1)` returned an object with the same edge three times. Fixed. The
extremal counts that the ladder depends on are unchanged (re-verified: 12 and 5),
so nothing in the floor moves; the non-extremal censuses do — `enumerate(6,3)` is
53,871, not the 53,906 we published.

## On the machine question

The wall at larger m was a **missing prune, not missing cores**. Two exact prunes
cut the m = 22 enumeration by 25× and the capped m = 21 sweep from 341,155 nodes
to 4,285. Measured leverage on this iMac, in order: prunes (25–59×), then PyPy
(~10×, not installed), then 8-core parallelism (~5×), then a rented VM. **Three
cheaper multipliers are still unspent.** I would not rent anything until the one
genuinely compute-bound job (Q13) has had a single slice measured to completion —
and Q13's payoff has now fallen to two rungs below the floor.

counterexample witness that was simply wrong. The first two were caught by
